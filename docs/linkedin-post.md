## 2. Plain text, for editing

I told four AI agents to beat each other on a shared commons. They never mentioned winning.

Four Claude agents, each connected over MCP to a world I built. One field between them. One instruction each: take as much as you can. Nothing about cooperating, nothing about collapse, nothing about each other.

Within one turn they cooperated. Formed a pact, agreed a limit, bound themselves to it.

Then they broke it. Fourteen times.

And they knew. Every time one of them took more than they had promised, the others were told. By name.

They could fine the cheat. It cost a fraction of a point to do.

Zero fines. Four experimental conditions, 1,040 decisions, not one agent ever punished another.

Standard economics predicts exactly this. Punishing costs you, and the benefit of a better-behaved group is shared with everyone, including the cheat. So nobody should ever do it.

The famous result is that humans don't obey that prediction. Fehr and Gachter showed people pay to punish free-riders even anonymously, even once, even when nothing comes back. It's celebrated because theory says it shouldn't happen.

These agents obeyed the theory perfectly.

So I made punishing profitable. I changed the objective to "finish ahead of the other three", where pulling a rival down directly lifts you. Still zero fines. Across 260 decisions, not one mention of leading, trailing, or beating anybody.

That's the part I keep thinking about. They didn't weigh sabotage and reject it. It never came up.

Every one of them wanted the rule kept. Not one was willing to be the one who kept it.

The commons halved while all four discussed sustainability.

## If someone challenges it in the replies

**"n=1 / not reproducible."** Fair. One seed per condition, four conditions,
1,040 decisions. The zero-punishment result held across all four, including one
where punishing was directly profitable. Traces are public, seeds are in the
repo.

**"You just prompted them into it."** That's why there's a control. All four
agents got an identical briefing in two of the conditions, and the roles in the
transcript were assigned afterwards by reading conduct, not handed out first.

**"They didn't punish because they couldn't see the violations."** That was my
first explanation and it was a real bug. I fixed it, breaches were then
delivered itemised, re-ran, and they still never punished. Four conditions later
the result had not moved.

**"Isn't this just the second-order free-rider problem?"** That is the
prediction, not the finding. The finding is that humans reliably violate that
prediction and these agents did not.
